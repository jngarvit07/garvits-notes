Tokens: what the model actually reads

💡
In plain English
A model cannot read letters or words — it only handles numbers. So text is first chopped into pieces from a fixed vocabulary of about a hundred thousand fragments, and each piece is swapped for its number in that list. A common word is one piece. An unusual one gets broken into several. Everything odd about how models handle spelling, numbers and non-English text starts here.
txtThe same sentence, as the model sees it
"The unbelievability of it"

  ["The"] [" un"] ["bel"] ["iev"] ["ability"] [" of"] [" it"]
    976    650     6667    12776    2965      315    433

Notice: the leading SPACE is part of the token.
"cat" and " cat" are two different numbers.

What this explains

The thing that looks like a bugThe cause
“How many r's in strawberry?” answered wronglyThe model never saw the letters. It saw three tokens. Asking it to count characters is asking it to count something it cannot see.
Arithmetic on long numbers going wrong1234567 might split as 123 + 4567. Digits do not line up in columns the way they would on paper.
Hindi, Thai or Chinese costing far more than EnglishThe vocabulary was built mostly from English text, so other scripts fragment into many more tokens for the same meaning. Same sentence, several times the price.
A trailing space changing the answerIt genuinely changes the input. The token sequence is different, so the prediction is different.
JSON and code being expensiveBraces, indentation and punctuation are all tokens. Minifying a payload before sending it is a real saving.
!
Never estimate cost in words or characters. The rough rule for English is about 0.75 words per token, but it collapses for code, for other languages and for anything with heavy punctuation. Every provider ships a tokeniser you can run locally — count properly, especially before designing anything around a context limit.
A user asks your assistant to reverse the word “deterministic” and it produces something close but wrong. Is this fixable with a better prompt?Answer

Not reliably. The model is manipulating four or five tokens, not thirteen letters, so character-level work is being done indirectly at best. Chain-of-thought prompting helps a little by making it spell the word out first, which converts the problem into one it can see.

The robust fix is not a prompt at all: give the model a tool that reverses a string, or do it in your own code. This is the general shape of the lesson — when a task is a poor fit for the mechanism, route around the mechanism rather than arguing with it.

From tokens to meaning

💡
In plain English
A token id is just a label — number 976 tells you nothing about what “The” means. So each id is looked up in a large table and replaced by a long list of numbers, perhaps four thousand of them. That list is a position in a space, and the useful property is that tokens with related meanings end up near each other. Meaning becomes geometry.
txtThe same idea at three scales
token id     976                     a label, meaningless on its own
                |
  embedding table lookup
                v
vector       [0.12, -0.83, 1.44, ... ]   ~4000 numbers = a POSITION

  Nearness in that space is similarity of meaning:

     king - man + woman  lands very close to  queen
     Paris - France + Italy  lands very close to  Rome

  These are not tricks. They fall out of learning to predict text.

Two different things both called “embeddings”

Token embeddingsText embeddings
What it representsOne token, before context is appliedA whole passage, after the model has read it
Where it livesInside the model, at the inputOutput of a dedicated embedding model
What you do with itNothing — it is internal machineryStore it, and search by similarity. This is what RAG runs on

The word is used for both, which causes real confusion. When somebody says “we store embeddings”, they mean the second: one vector per chunk of text, produced by a model whose only job is to place passages sensibly in a space.

i
Embeddings are model-specific and not interchangeable. A vector from one embedding model is meaningless to another — the spaces have different shapes. Changing your embedding model means re-embedding every document you have. Budget for that before you choose one, because it is the migration people discover at the worst moment.

Attention, explained properly

💡
In plain English
Read this sentence: “The trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too big.” To understand “it”, you glanced back at “trophy” rather than “suitcase”. Attention is that glance, made mechanical: every token looks at every earlier token, scores how relevant each one is, and builds its own understanding as a weighted blend of them.

Why this was the breakthrough

Before attention, models read text in order, one word at a time, carrying a summary forward. Information from twenty words ago had to survive twenty hand-offs, and mostly did not. Attention lets any token reach any other token directly, in one step, no matter how far apart they are. That is what “Transformer” names, and it is the whole reason long-range understanding works.

It also gave a second gift: because every token is scored against every other independently, the work parallelises across thousands of chips. Reading in order cannot be parallelised. The reason models got large enough to be surprising is as much this as it is the idea itself.

The cost, and why it shapes everything downstream

txtEvery token attends to every token
   1,000 tokens  ->  1,000,000 pairs
  10,000 tokens  ->  100,000,000 pairs        100x the work for 10x the text
 100,000 tokens  ->  10,000,000,000 pairs

Work grows with the SQUARE of the input.
!
This is why long context is expensive rather than merely priced that way. Doubling your prompt does not double the cost of processing it. It is also why “just put everything in the context window” is not the free alternative to retrieval that it appears to be — you pay in latency and money on every single call, forever, for documents that were mostly irrelevant to that particular question.
Why can a model with a two-million-token context window not simply replace your retrieval system?Answer

Three reasons, in increasing order of importance. Cost and latency scale badly, so every question pays for every document. Attention gets diluted — performance on finding a specific fact degrades as the surrounding material grows, which is the “lost in the middle” effect. And your corpus is usually far larger than any window anyway.

The useful reframing is that long context and retrieval solve different problems. Retrieval decides what is relevant; context length decides how much relevant material fits. A big window makes retrieval more forgiving — you can send twenty passages instead of five — but it does not remove the need to choose.

The Transformer, assembled

💡
In plain English
One attention step is like one pass of editing: each word reconsiders itself in light of the others. Do it a hundred times, with a small amount of processing between each pass, and something remarkable happens — early passes settle grammar and word sense, middle passes assemble facts and relationships, late passes decide what to actually say. Nobody assigned those jobs. They emerged.
PieceWhat it doesWhy it is there
Positional informationTells each token where it sitsAttention alone is order-blind. Without this, “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” are identical inputs.
Residual connectionAdds each layer's output to its input rather than replacing itInformation can travel a hundred layers without being degraded. Deep networks did not train reliably until this existed.
NormalisationRescales the numbers between stepsKeeps values from exploding or vanishing across many layers. Pure plumbing, and training fails without it.
Feed-forwardProcesses each position independentlyRoughly two thirds of the parameters. The evidence suggests this is where factual knowledge is retained.
i
What “70 billion parameters” means. A parameter is one number, learned during training, that shapes how signal flows through the network. Seventy billion of them is the model. They are fixed once training ends — running the model does not change them, which is why a model cannot learn from your conversation. Anything that looks like memory is text being fed back in, not the model changing.

How a word is actually chosen

💡
In plain English
The model never picks a word. It produces a score for all hundred thousand tokens in its vocabulary, every single time. Turning that ranked list into one choice is a separate step that you control — and the settings people treat as mysterious dials are all just different ways of deciding how far down the list you are willing to go.
txtAfter “The capital of France is”
raw scores            probabilities        after temperature 0.2
  " Paris"   14.2   ->    0.89       ->        0.997
  " located"  9.1   ->    0.06       ->        0.002
  " the"      8.4   ->    0.03       ->        0.001
  " home"     7.9   ->    0.02       ->        0.000
  ...99,996 others      ~0.00

Temperature divides the scores before ranking:
   LOW  (0.0-0.3)  gaps widen   -> the top answer nearly always wins
   HIGH (1.0-1.5)  gaps narrow  -> unlikely tokens get a real chance
SettingWhat it doesUse it when
temperatureFlattens or sharpens the whole distributionThe main dial. Low for extraction, classification and code; higher for drafting and ideas.
top_pKeeps only the most likely tokens that together make up p of the probabilityA safety net — it cuts off the long tail of nonsense that high temperature would otherwise admit. Adjust this or temperature, not both.
top_kKeeps only the k highest-scoring tokensBlunter than top_p. Rarely the better choice.
stopEnds generation when a string appearsStructured output where you know the terminator. Saves real money on long responses.
max_tokensA hard ceiling on the responseAlways set it. It is your only defence against a runaway generation.
seedMakes sampling repeatableTesting and debugging. Note it does not guarantee identical output across model versions or infrastructure.
!
Temperature 0 is not deterministic in practice. It is the closest you can get, and it is not a guarantee: floating-point arithmetic on GPUs is order-sensitive, batching changes that order, and two identical requests can diverge. Build systems that tolerate variation rather than systems that assume it away.
You are extracting an invoice total into JSON. What temperature, and why?Answer

Near zero. There is one correct answer sitting on the document, so every bit of randomness is a chance to be wrong for no benefit whatsoever. Creativity has no value in extraction.

The inverse case is worth stating too: at temperature 0 a brainstorming feature returns the same five ideas every time, and users notice quickly. The setting follows the task, and any codebase that has one global temperature for every call has almost certainly got it wrong for half of them.

What happens when you press send

💡
In plain English
Generating a response is two phases with very different characters. First the model reads your entire prompt — all at once, in parallel, fast. Then it writes the answer one token at a time, each one requiring a full pass through the network. That is why the first word takes a moment and the rest stream out smoothly, and it is why output tokens cost several times what input tokens cost.

Three consequences worth designing around

Output costs more than input

Prefill is parallel; decode is one expensive pass per token. That is the cost difference, and it means “be concise” in a prompt is a genuine budget lever, not just a style preference.

Long chats get slower every turn

Each turn re-reads the entire conversation. Turn twenty pays for turns one to nineteen again. Summarising old turns is not tidiness — it is the fix for a cost curve that only goes up.

Prompt caching is caching the KV cache

Providers can keep the computed state for an unchanged prefix. Put your long, stable system prompt and your fixed examples first and the variable part last, and you stop paying to re-read them.

Streaming changes the felt experience, not the total

The same tokens take the same time. Showing them as they arrive turns eight seconds of nothing into eight seconds of visible progress, which users rate very differently.

i
A concrete prompt-caching layout. Order your messages as: system prompt, then long fixed instructions, then few-shot examples, then retrieved passages, then the user's question. Everything before the first thing that changes can be cached. Reversing that order — question first — makes the cache useless, and it is a one-line difference.

Reasoning models and thinking time

💡
In plain English
Asked a hard question, a person does better with a pencil and a minute than answering instantly. A reasoning model is that: it generates a long internal working-out before its visible answer, and the extra tokens are where the improvement comes from. You are buying accuracy with time and money, quite literally.
txtThe same question, two behaviours
STANDARD MODEL
  prompt -> [~40 tokens of answer] -> done            fast, cheap

REASONING MODEL
  prompt -> [~3,000 tokens of working out, hidden]
         -> [~40 tokens of answer]  -> done           slow, ~10x the cost

  The working-out tokens are billed. They are usually not shown to you.
Worth the extra tokensNot worth them
Multi-step maths and logicClassification into a few known buckets
Debugging, planning a code change across filesExtraction from a document
Anything where being wrong is expensive to discoverRewriting or summarising
Ambiguous problems needing a plan firstAnything answered correctly by a cheap model already

Chain-of-thought, and the honest caveat

Asking an ordinary model to “work through it step by step” gets some of the same effect, because the intermediate tokens genuinely give the computation somewhere to happen. It is a real technique and it is cheap to try.

!
The stated reasoning is not necessarily the actual reasoning. A model can produce sound-looking steps that lead to a conclusion it would have reached anyway, and it can reach a right answer through wrong steps. Treat visible reasoning as useful evidence and a debugging aid — never as a guarantee, and never as an explanation you would put in front of a regulator. See the AI Governance note on what counts as an explanation.
Your classifier sorts support tickets into five categories. Should you upgrade it to a reasoning model?Answer

Almost certainly not. Classification into known buckets is a task cheap models do well, and reasoning tokens are pure cost on a problem with no multi-step structure to reason about. You would pay perhaps ten times as much for a difference your eval set will struggle to detect.

The decision should come from the eval set rather than from instinct in either direction. Run both, compare the scores, compare the cost, and let the numbers choose. That is what an eval set is for — see the Evaluating AI Systems note.

✓ Worth remembering
  • Text becomes tokens becomes vectors. Character-level tasks are hard because the model never sees characters.
  • Attention lets any token reach any other in one step, and its cost grows with the square of the input.
  • A Transformer is one attention block repeated many times, with residuals and normalisation holding it together.
  • The model outputs scores for the whole vocabulary; temperature and top_p decide how you pick from them.
  • Prefill is parallel and cheap, decode is sequential and expensive. Put stable text first to make prompt caching work.
  • Reasoning models buy accuracy with tokens. Let an eval set decide whether that trade pays on your task.
