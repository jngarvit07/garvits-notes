Tokens: what the model actually reads

💡
In plain English
A model cannot read letters or words — it only handles numbers. So the text is first cut into pieces taken from a fixed list of about one lakh fragments, and each piece is swapped for its number in that list. A common word is one piece. An unusual word gets broken into several. Everything strange about how models handle spelling, numbers and non-English text starts right here.
txtThe same sentence, as the model sees it
"The unbelievability of it"

  ["The"] [" un"] ["bel"] ["iev"] ["ability"] [" of"] [" it"]
    976    650     6667    12776    2965      315    433

Notice: the SPACE in front is part of the token.
"cat" and " cat" are two different numbers.

What this explains

The thing that looks like a bugThe reason
"How many r's in strawberry?" answered wronglyThe model never saw the letters. It saw three tokens. Asking it to count letters is asking it to count something it cannot see.
Sums on long numbers going wrong1234567 might split as 123 + 4567. The digits do not line up in columns the way they would on paper.
Hindi, Tamil or Chinese costing far more than EnglishThe word list was built mostly from English text, so other scripts break into many more tokens for the same meaning. Same sentence, several times the price.
An extra space at the end changing the answerIt genuinely changes the input. The tokens are different, so the prediction is different.
JSON and code being expensiveBrackets, indentation and punctuation are all tokens. Squeezing out the extra spaces before sending is a real saving.
!
Never guess your cost from words or letters. The rough rule for English is about 0.75 words per token, but it falls apart for code, for other languages and for anything with a lot of punctuation. Every provider gives you a tokeniser you can run on your own machine — count properly, especially before you design anything around a context limit.
A user asks your assistant to reverse the word “deterministic” and it gives something close but wrong. Can a better prompt fix this?Answer

Not reliably. The model is moving four or five tokens around, not thirteen letters, so letter-level work is happening indirectly at best. Asking it to think step by step helps a little, because it spells the word out first, which turns the problem into one it can actually see.

The solid fix is not a prompt at all: give the model a tool that reverses a string, or just do it in your own code. That is the general lesson — when a task does not suit the machinery, go around the machinery instead of arguing with it.

From tokens to meaning

💡
In plain English
A token id is only a label — the number 976 tells you nothing about what "The" means. So each id is looked up in a big table and replaced by a long list of numbers, maybe four thousand of them. That list is a position in a space, and the useful part is that tokens with related meanings end up close to each other. Meaning turns into geometry.
txtThe same idea at three levels
token id     976                     a label, meaningless on its own
                |
  embedding table lookup
                v
vector       [0.12, -0.83, 1.44, ... ]   ~4000 numbers = a POSITION

  Being close in that space means being close in meaning:

     king - man + woman  lands very close to  queen
     Paris - France + Italy  lands very close to  Rome

  These are not tricks. They simply fall out of learning to predict text.

Two different things, both called “embeddings”

Token embeddingsText embeddings
What it stands forOne token, before any context is appliedA whole passage, after the model has read it
Where it livesInside the model, right at the inputThe output of a separate embedding model
What you do with itNothing — it is internal machineryStore it, and search by similarity. This is what RAG runs on

The same word is used for both, which causes real confusion. When somebody says "we store embeddings", they mean the second one: one vector per chunk of text, produced by a model whose only job is to place passages sensibly in that space.

i
Embeddings belong to one model and cannot be swapped between models. A vector from one embedding model means nothing to another one — the two spaces are shaped differently. Changing your embedding model means re-embedding every document you have. Plan for that before you pick one, because it is the migration people discover at the worst possible moment.

Attention, explained properly

💡
In plain English
Read this sentence: "The trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too big." To understand "it", your eye went back to "trophy", not to "suitcase". Attention is that glance, turned into machinery: every token looks at every earlier token, gives each one a score for how relevant it is, and builds its own understanding as a mixture of them.

Why this was the breakthrough

Before attention, models read text in order, one word at a time, carrying a summary forward. Information from twenty words back had to survive twenty hand-offs, and mostly it did not. Attention lets any token reach any other token directly, in one step, however far apart they are. That is what the word "Transformer" refers to, and it is the whole reason models can follow long passages.

It brought a second gift too. Because every token is scored against every other one separately, the work can be split across thousands of chips at the same time. Reading in order cannot be split that way. The reason models got big enough to surprise us is as much this as it is the idea itself.

The cost, and why it shapes everything after it

txtEvery token looks at every token
   1,000 tokens  ->  1,000,000 pairs
  10,000 tokens  ->  100,000,000 pairs        100x the work for 10x the text
 100,000 tokens  ->  10,000,000,000 pairs

The work grows with the SQUARE of the input.
!
This is why long context is genuinely expensive, not just priced that way. Doubling your prompt does not double the work of reading it. It is also why "just put everything in the context window" is not the free alternative to retrieval that it looks like — you pay in waiting time and money on every single call, forever, for documents that had nothing to do with that particular question.
Why can a model with a two-million-token context window not simply replace your retrieval system?Answer

Three reasons, from smallest to biggest. Cost and speed get much worse, so every question pays for every document. Attention gets spread thin — the model gets worse at finding one specific fact as the surrounding material grows, which is the "lost in the middle" problem. And your pile of documents is usually far bigger than any window anyway.

The useful way to look at it is that long context and retrieval solve different problems. Retrieval decides what is relevant; the context length decides how much of that relevant material fits. A big window makes retrieval more forgiving — you can send twenty passages instead of five — but it does not remove the need to choose.

The Transformer, assembled

💡
In plain English
One attention step is like one round of editing: each word rethinks itself in the light of the others. Do that a hundred times, with a little processing between each round, and something remarkable happens — early rounds settle the grammar and word meanings, middle rounds put together facts and relationships, late rounds decide what to actually say. Nobody handed out those jobs. They appeared on their own.
PieceWhat it doesWhy it is there
Positional informationTells each token where it sits in the lineAttention on its own is blind to order. Without this, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" are the same input.
Residual connectionAdds each layer's output to its input instead of replacing itInformation can travel through a hundred layers without getting damaged. Deep networks would not train properly until this existed.
NormalisationRescales the numbers between stepsStops values from blowing up or shrinking to nothing across many layers. Pure plumbing, and training fails without it.
Feed-forwardProcesses each position on its ownRoughly two thirds of all the parameters. The evidence suggests this is where factual knowledge is kept.
i
What "70 billion parameters" means. A parameter is one number, learned during training, that shapes how the signal flows through the network. Seventy billion of them together are the model. They are frozen once training ends — running the model does not change them, which is why a model cannot learn anything from your conversation. Anything that looks like memory is just text being fed back in, not the model changing.

How a word is actually chosen

💡
In plain English
The model never picks a word. It produces a score for all one lakh tokens in its vocabulary, every single time. Turning that ranked list into one choice is a separate step that you control — and the settings people treat as mysterious dials are all just different ways of deciding how far down the list you are willing to go.
txtAfter “The capital of France is”
raw scores            probabilities        after temperature 0.2
  " Paris"   14.2   ->    0.89       ->        0.997
  " located"  9.1   ->    0.06       ->        0.002
  " the"      8.4   ->    0.03       ->        0.001
  " home"     7.9   ->    0.02       ->        0.000
  ...99,996 others      ~0.00

Temperature divides the scores before ranking:
   LOW  (0.0-0.3)  gaps get wider   -> the top answer almost always wins
   HIGH (1.0-1.5)  gaps get smaller -> unlikely tokens get a real chance
SettingWhat it doesUse it when
temperatureFlattens or sharpens the whole spread of scoresThe main dial. Low for pulling out data, sorting and code; higher for drafting and ideas.
top_pKeeps only the most likely tokens that together add up to p of the chanceA safety net — it cuts off the long tail of nonsense that a high temperature would otherwise let in. Adjust this or temperature, not both.
top_kKeeps only the k highest-scoring tokensRougher than top_p. Rarely the better choice.
stopStops the writing when a certain string appearsStructured output where you know the ending marker. Saves real money on long answers.
max_tokensA hard limit on the answer lengthAlways set it. It is your only protection against an answer that runs away.
seedMakes the random picking repeatableTesting and debugging. Note that it does not promise identical output across model versions or different machines.
!
Temperature 0 is not truly fixed in practice. It is as close as you can get, and it is not a promise. Rounding in GPU maths depends on the order of operations, grouping requests together changes that order, and two identical requests can drift apart. Build systems that can live with small variations rather than systems that assume they cannot happen.
You are pulling an invoice total out into JSON. What temperature, and why?Answer

Close to zero. There is exactly one correct answer sitting on the document, so every bit of randomness is a chance to be wrong with no upside at all. Creativity is worth nothing when you are extracting a number.

The opposite case is worth saying too: at temperature 0 a brainstorming feature returns the same five ideas every time, and users spot that very quickly. The setting follows the task, and any codebase with one global temperature for every call has almost certainly got it wrong for half of them.

What happens when you press send

💡
In plain English
Producing an answer happens in two phases that feel very different. First the model reads your whole prompt — all at once, in parallel, quickly. Then it writes the answer one token at a time, and each token needs a full pass through the network. That is why the first word takes a moment and the rest flow out smoothly, and it is why output tokens cost several times what input tokens cost.

Three results worth designing around

Output costs more than input

Prefill happens all at once; decode is one expensive pass per token. That is where the price difference comes from, and it means "keep it short" in a prompt is a real budget lever, not just a matter of taste.

Long chats get slower every turn

Each turn re-reads the whole conversation. Turn twenty pays for turns one to nineteen all over again. Summarising the old turns is not housekeeping — it is the fix for a cost curve that only climbs.

Prompt caching is caching the KV cache

Providers can hold on to the worked-out state for an opening block that has not changed. Put your long, unchanging system prompt and fixed examples first and the changing part last, and you stop paying to re-read them.

Streaming changes how it feels, not the total time

The same tokens take the same time. Showing them as they arrive turns eight seconds of nothing into eight seconds of visible progress, and users judge those two very differently.

i
A concrete prompt-caching layout. Order your messages like this: system prompt, then long fixed instructions, then your examples, then the fetched passages, then the user's question. Everything before the first thing that changes can be cached. Reversing that order — question first — makes the cache useless, and it is a one-line difference.

Reasoning models and thinking time

💡
In plain English
Given a hard question, a person does better with a rough sheet and one minute than by answering instantly. A reasoning model is exactly that: it writes out a long rough working before its visible answer, and those extra tokens are where the improvement comes from. You are buying accuracy with time and money, quite literally.
txtThe same question, two behaviours
STANDARD MODEL
  prompt -> [~40 tokens of answer] -> done            fast, cheap

REASONING MODEL
  prompt -> [~3,000 tokens of rough working, hidden]
         -> [~40 tokens of answer]  -> done           slow, ~10x the cost

  The rough-working tokens are charged for. You usually never see them.
Worth the extra tokensNot worth them
Multi-step maths and logicSorting into a few known buckets
Debugging, planning a code change across many filesPulling fields out of a document
Anything where a mistake is expensive to spot laterRewriting or summarising
Unclear problems that need a plan firstAnything a cheap model already answers correctly

Chain-of-thought, and the honest warning

Asking an ordinary model to "work through it step by step" gets you some of the same benefit, because those in-between tokens genuinely give the thinking somewhere to happen. It is a real technique and it costs nothing to try.

!
The reasoning it shows you is not necessarily the reasoning it used. A model can write out sensible-looking steps leading to a conclusion it would have reached anyway, and it can also reach a right answer through wrong steps. Treat visible reasoning as useful evidence and a debugging aid — never as a guarantee, and never as an explanation you would hand to a regulator. See the AI Governance note on what actually counts as an explanation.
Your classifier sorts support tickets into five categories. Should you upgrade it to a reasoning model?Answer

Almost certainly not. Sorting into known buckets is something cheap models do well, and reasoning tokens are pure cost on a problem that has no multi-step structure to reason about. You would pay maybe ten times as much for a difference your test set will struggle to even detect.

The decision should come from the test set, not from gut feeling in either direction. Run both, compare the scores, compare the cost, and let the numbers choose. That is what a test set is for — see the Evaluating AI Systems note.

✓ Worth remembering
  • Text becomes tokens, tokens become vectors. Letter-level tasks are hard because the model never sees letters.
  • Attention lets any token reach any other in one step, and its cost grows with the square of the input.
  • A Transformer is one attention block repeated many times, held together by residuals and normalisation.
  • The model gives scores for the whole vocabulary; temperature and top_p decide how you pick from them.
  • Prefill happens all at once and is cheap; decode happens in order and is expensive. Put unchanging text first so prompt caching works.
  • Reasoning models buy accuracy with tokens. Let a test set decide whether that trade is worth it for your task.
