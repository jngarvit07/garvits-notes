What fine-tuning can and cannot teach

💡
In plain English
Fine-tuning is like showing someone a thousand solved examples until they pick up the house style. It is very good at teaching how to reply — the format, the tone, the shape of a good answer. It is bad at teaching what is true, because facts change and retraining is slow, costly, and cannot be done every Tuesday when a policy gets updated.
Your actual problemThe answerWhy
"It does not know about our products"RetrievalThose are facts, and facts change. Update a document, not a model.
"It will not stick to our output format"Structured output firstA schema forces this for free. Try that before you train anything.
"It ignores an instruction we keep repeating"Prompt work firstUsually a half-written brief rather than a weak model.
"Our prompt is 4,000 tokens of examples and it is slow and costly"Fine-tuningA genuine fit. Bake the examples in, then send a short prompt.
"We need this exact tone and no amount of prompting gets it"Fine-tuningStyle is exactly what it teaches well.
"A small model would do if it just knew this one narrow task"Fine-tuning (distillation)The strongest money argument there is. Covered below.
"It makes things up"Neither, on its ownFine-tuning on your data can make it make things up more confidently, in your own house style. Retrieval plus checking is the answer.
!
Fine-tuning on your documents does not put those documents inside the model. This is the most expensive misunderstanding in the field. Training on your knowledge base teaches the model to sound like your knowledge base — the tone, the vocabulary, the structure — while it stays just as capable of inventing a policy nobody ever wrote. It often makes things worse, because the invented answers now come in your house voice and read as official.
A team fine-tunes on 5,000 support tickets so the assistant “knows their product”. What will actually happen?Answer

It will learn to write like their support team — the greetings, the structure, the vocabulary, the sign-off. Some facts will stick by accident, unreliably, and with no way to tell which ones. When the return policy changes next month, the model will keep confidently stating the old one, and there is no document you can edit to fix it.

Those same 5,000 tickets used as a set of documents to search would give current, citable, updatable answers. And if the tone genuinely matters too, do both — fine-tune for the voice, retrieve for the facts. They are not competing options; they solve different halves of the problem.

Full, LoRA, QLoRA

💡
In plain English
A model is billions of numbers. Full fine-tuning changes all of them, which needs huge hardware and produces a huge new file. LoRA leaves them frozen and instead trains a small patch that sits alongside — usually under 1% of the size — and for most tasks that works almost as well. It is the difference between reprinting a whole book and writing notes in the margins.

i
The multi-customer benefit is the one people miss. Because an adapter is small and the base model is shared, one running model can hold dozens of adapters in memory and pick the right one per request. Having a different tuned behaviour for each customer becomes financially normal instead of ridiculous — with full fine-tuning it would mean one entire model per customer.

The data is the whole job

💡
In plain English
Fine-tuning copies whatever is in your examples, including the mistakes and the disagreements. If three people wrote your training data and they each format dates differently, the model learns to be inconsistent about dates — and it learns that very faithfully. This is why quality beats quantity so heavily, and why most failed fine-tuning projects failed at the dataset, not at the training.
jsonlThe format, and the rule about system prompts
{"messages":[
  {"role":"system","content":"You are the Acme support assistant."},
  {"role":"user","content":"My order hasn't arrived."},
  {"role":"assistant","content":"I'm sorry about that. Could you share your order number so I can check the delivery status?"}
]}
{"messages":[
  {"role":"system","content":"You are the Acme support assistant."},
  {"role":"user","content":"Where's my refund?"},
  {"role":"assistant","content":"Refunds reach your account 5-10 working days after approval. Could you share your order number so I can confirm it was processed?"}
]}

RULES THAT MATTER
  - Use the SAME system prompt in training as you will use live.
    A mismatch is the most common cause of "but it was fine in testing".
  - Every example must be an answer you would be happy to send to a customer.
    The model copies your average, not your best.
  - Keep 10-20% aside as a validation set BEFORE you look at any of it.
How many do you need?

50–100 to see whether it is going to help at all. 500–1,000 for solid results on format and tone. 5,000+ for a genuinely new skill. Adding more inconsistent examples beyond that does not help and can make things worse.

Where they should come from

Real conversations, corrected by somebody who knows the right answer. Data generated by a bigger model is a fair approach and it works — but your quality can never go above that model's, and any bias it has becomes yours permanently.

Include the hard cases

Unclear requests, ones that should be refused, ones that need a clarifying question back. A dataset of clean easy cases produces a model that is confidently wrong the moment real life gets messy.

Remove duplicates, properly

Near-duplicates make the model give too much weight to whatever they contain. Remove them by embedding similarity, not by exact text match — the copies are rarely word for word.

!
Check the licence and the personal data before you train. Training bakes the content in permanently: you cannot delete one customer's data out of a trained model, which puts a deletion request in direct conflict with the thing you built. Remove personal data before training, keep a record of what went in, and read the AI Governance note before using customer conversations as training data.

Preference tuning: RLHF and DPO

💡
In plain English
Ordinary fine-tuning needs one right answer for each input. But for "write a polite refusal" there is no single right answer — there are only better and worse ones. Preference tuning trains on pairs: here are two replies, this one is better. It is how a raw text-predictor becomes an assistant that is helpful and says no sensibly, and it is a big part of what separates a base model from a chat model.
jsonlA preference pair
{"prompt":  "A customer is angry their order is late. Reply.",
 "chosen":  "I'm really sorry — that's frustrating. I can see it left our warehouse on the 3rd and is delayed in transit. I've requested an update and will email you within 24 hours. If you'd rather not wait, I can refund it now.",
 "rejected":"Unfortunately delays can occur due to circumstances beyond our control. Please consult our shipping policy for further information."}

Both are on-topic and grammatically fine. One is far better.
That judgement is what you are teaching, and it is very hard to
write down as a rule — which is exactly why pairs work so well.

You probably already have preference data. Every thumbs-up and thumbs-down, every time a user edited a generated draft before sending it, every time a support agent rewrote a suggested reply. The edited version is the chosen one and the original is rejected. Capturing that from day one costs almost nothing and is worth a great deal later — see the AI Ops note on feedback signals.

Distillation, and the case that actually pays

💡
In plain English
Use the expensive model to do the job perfectly a few thousand times, then train a small cheap model on those answers. The small model will not match it in general — but on that one narrow task it can come very close, at a fraction of the cost and a fraction of the waiting time. This is where fine-tuning most reliably makes financial sense.
Real worldWhen it is worth it, and when it is not

Worth it: a classifier running on every incoming message, a few million times a month. The task is narrow, the volume is huge, and the user is waiting for it. A shrunk-down small model can be ten times cheaper and several times faster, and the test set tells you whether the quality held up.

Not worth it: a feature called two hundred times a day. The engineering, the retraining as the task drifts, the serving setup and one more thing to watch all cost more than the tokens you would save. Fine-tuning has a fixed cost that never goes away — a model you must re-tune whenever the task changes, and a pipeline somebody has to own.

The honest test is arithmetic. Take your current monthly token spend on that one feature, work out the saving, and compare it with a week of engineering plus ongoing upkeep. If the answer is not obvious, the answer is no.

Why filter the teacher model’s answers before training on them?Answer

Because the teacher is not perfect, and the student copies everything it is shown with no way to tell good examples from bad. Training on unfiltered output bakes the teacher's error rate into the student permanently — and the student can never do better than what it was taught.

Filtering with your own checks, your business rules, and ideally a sample checked by a human, is what makes the student's ceiling the teacher's good answers instead of its average ones.

Living with a fine-tuned model

💡
In plain English
The training run is the short part. What follows is a permanent duty: something that has to be versioned, tested, served, watched, and eventually retrained when the task drifts or the base model is retired. A fine-tuned model is a dependency you now maintain, and that cost should be part of the decision from the very start.
WhatBecause
Version the dataset along with the modelOtherwise "which examples caused this behaviour?" cannot be answered, and it is the first question when something goes wrong.
Record the base model, the settings, and the commitSo you can rebuild it. A tuned model you cannot rebuild is a liability with an expiry date.
Run the same test set as the base modelThe only way to state the improvement as a number instead of a feeling.
Watch for general ability getting worseTraining hard on one task damages others — catastrophic forgetting. Keep a few general cases in the test set to catch it.
Keep the base model as a fallbackOne flag takes you back to something that works if the tuned one misbehaves in production.
Put the base model's retirement date in your calendarYour adapter is tied to one specific base model. When it retires, you retrain — and you want that planned, not sprung on you.
!
Re-run your tests whenever the base model updates. An adapter trained against one version of a base model can behave differently on the next one, in ways nobody deployed and nobody expected. This is the same argument as pinning model versions in the AI Ops note, with more at stake: here the thing that shifted under your feet is something you paid to build.
✓ Worth remembering
  • Fine-tuning teaches manner, not facts. Facts belong in retrieval.
  • Try prompting and structured output first. Most fine-tuning projects should have stopped there.
  • Use LoRA for nearly everything: about 1% of the parameters, a small adapter, many versions on one base.
  • The dataset is the job. 500–1,000 consistent examples beat 50,000 inconsistent ones.
  • Prefer DPO over RLHF if you need preference tuning, and you may already be collecting the pairs.
  • Distillation is the strongest money argument — but do the sum before, not after.
  • A tuned model is a versioned dependency you now maintain. Put that cost in the decision.
