What fine-tuning can and cannot teach

💡
In plain English
Fine-tuning is showing someone a thousand worked examples until they pick up the house style. It is very good at teaching how to respond — the format, the tone, the shape of a good answer. It is poor at teaching what is true, because facts change and retraining is slow, expensive and cannot be done every Tuesday when a policy is updated.
Your actual problemThe answerWhy
“It does not know about our products”RetrievalFacts, and they change. Update a document, not a model.
“It will not stick to our output format”Structured output firstA schema enforces this for free. Try it before training anything.
“It ignores an instruction we keep repeating”Prompt work firstUsually an underspecified brief rather than an incapable model.
“Our prompt is 4,000 tokens of examples and it is slow and costly”Fine-tuningGenuine fit. Bake the examples in, send a short prompt.
“We need this tone and no amount of prompting gets it”Fine-tuningStyle is exactly what it teaches well.
“A small model would do if it just knew this narrow task”Fine-tuning (distillation)The strongest economic case there is. Covered below.
“It hallucinates”Neither, aloneFine-tuning on your data can make it hallucinate more confidently in your house style. Retrieval plus validation is the answer.
!
Fine-tuning on documents does not put those documents in the model. This is the most expensive misunderstanding in the field. Training on your knowledge base teaches the model to sound like your knowledge base — the register, the vocabulary, the structure — while remaining just as capable of inventing a policy that was never written. It often makes things worse, because the invented answers now carry your house voice and read as authoritative.
A team fine-tunes on 5,000 support tickets so the assistant “knows their product”. What will actually happen?Answer

It will learn to write like their support team — the greetings, the structure, the vocabulary, the sign-off. Some facts will stick incidentally, unreliably and without any way to tell which. When the return policy changes next month, the model will keep confidently stating the old one, and there is no document to edit.

The same 5,000 tickets used as a retrieval corpus would give current, citable, updatable answers. And if the tone genuinely matters too, do both — fine-tune for the voice, retrieve for the facts. They are not alternatives; they solve different halves.

Full, LoRA, QLoRA

💡
In plain English
A model is billions of numbers. Full fine-tuning adjusts all of them, which needs enormous hardware and produces an enormous new file. LoRA leaves them frozen and trains a small patch that sits alongside — typically under 1% the size — which turns out to work nearly as well for most tasks. It is the difference between reprinting a book and adding annotations to the margins.

i
The multi-tenant property is the one people miss. Because an adapter is small and the base is shared, one served model can carry dozens of adapters in memory and pick per request. A different tuned behaviour per customer becomes economically ordinary rather than absurd — with full fine-tuning it would be one entire model per customer.

The data is the whole job

💡
In plain English
Fine-tuning copies whatever is in your examples, including the mistakes and the disagreements. If three people wrote your training data and they format dates differently, the model learns to be inconsistent about dates — and it learns that faithfully. This is why quality beats quantity so heavily, and why most failed fine-tuning projects failed at the dataset rather than the training.
jsonlThe format, and the rule about system prompts
{"messages":[
  {"role":"system","content":"You are the Acme support assistant."},
  {"role":"user","content":"My order hasn't arrived."},
  {"role":"assistant","content":"I'm sorry about that. Could you share your order number so I can check the delivery status?"}
]}
{"messages":[
  {"role":"system","content":"You are the Acme support assistant."},
  {"role":"user","content":"Where's my refund?"},
  {"role":"assistant","content":"Refunds reach your account 5-10 working days after approval. Could you share your order number so I can confirm it was processed?"}
]}

RULES THAT MATTER
  - Use the SAME system prompt in training as you will at inference.
    A mismatch is the most common cause of "it was fine in testing".
  - Every example must be an output you would be happy to ship.
    The model copies your average, not your best.
  - Hold back 10-20% as a validation set BEFORE you look at any of it.
How many do you need?

50–100 to tell whether it is going to help at all. 500–1,000 for solid results on format and tone. 5,000+ for a genuinely new skill. Adding more inconsistent examples past that point does not help and can hurt.

Where they should come from

Real interactions, corrected by someone who knows the right answer. Synthetic data generated by a larger model is legitimate and works — but it caps your quality at that model's, and any bias it has becomes yours permanently.

Include the hard cases

Ambiguous requests, ones that should be refused, ones needing a clarifying question. A dataset of clean happy paths produces a model that is confidently wrong the moment reality is untidy.

Deduplicate, properly

Near-duplicates make the model overweight whatever they contain. Deduplicate by embedding similarity, not by exact string match — the copies are rarely identical.

!
Check the licence and the personal data before you train. Training bakes the content in irreversibly: you cannot delete one customer's data out of a trained model, which puts a deletion request in direct conflict with the artefact. Redact personal data before training, keep a record of what went in, and read the AI Governance note before using customer conversations as training data.

Preference tuning: RLHF and DPO

💡
In plain English
Ordinary fine-tuning needs one right answer per input. But for “write a polite refusal” there is no single right answer — there are better and worse ones. Preference tuning trains on pairs: here are two responses, this one is better. It is how the raw text-predictor becomes an assistant that is helpful and declines sensibly, and it is a large part of what separates a base model from a chat model.
jsonlA preference pair
{"prompt":  "A customer is angry their order is late. Reply.",
 "chosen":  "I'm really sorry — that's frustrating. I can see it left our warehouse on the 3rd and is delayed in transit. I've requested an update and will email you within 24 hours. If you'd rather not wait, I can refund it now.",
 "rejected":"Unfortunately delays can occur due to circumstances beyond our control. Please consult our shipping policy for further information."}

Both are on-topic and grammatical. One is much better.

You probably have preference data already. Every thumbs-up and thumbs-down, every time a user edited a generated draft before sending it, every time a support agent rewrote a suggested reply. The edited version is chosen and the original is rejected. Capturing that from the start costs almost nothing and is worth a great deal later — see the AI Ops note on feedback signals.

Distillation, and the case that actually pays

💡
In plain English
Use the expensive model to do the job perfectly a few thousand times, then train a small cheap model on those answers. The small model will not match it in general — but on that one narrow task it can get very close, at a fraction of the cost and a fraction of the latency. This is where fine-tuning most reliably makes financial sense.
Real worldWhen it is worth it, and when it is not

Worth it: a classifier running on every inbound message, a few million times a month. The task is narrow, the volume is high, and latency is in the user's path. A distilled small model can be an order of magnitude cheaper and several times faster, and the eval set tells you whether quality held.

Not worth it: a feature called two hundred times a day. The engineering, the retraining as the task drifts, the serving infrastructure and the extra thing to monitor all cost more than the tokens you would save. Fine-tuning has a fixed cost that never goes away — a model you must re-tune whenever the task changes, and a pipeline somebody has to own.

The honest test is arithmetic. Take your current monthly token spend on that one feature, estimate the saving, and compare it to a week of engineering plus ongoing maintenance. If the answer is not obvious, it is a no.

Why filter the teacher model’s outputs before training on them?Answer

Because the teacher is not perfect, and the student copies everything it is shown with no way to tell good examples from bad. Training on unfiltered output bakes the teacher’s error rate into the student permanently — and the student cannot exceed what it was taught.

Filtering with your validators, your business rules and ideally a sample of human review is what makes the student’s ceiling the teacher’s good answers rather than its average ones.

Living with a fine-tuned model

💡
In plain English
The training run is the short part. What follows is a permanent obligation: an artefact that has to be versioned, evaluated, served, monitored and eventually retrained when the task drifts or the base model is deprecated. A fine-tuned model is a dependency you now maintain, and that cost should be in the decision from the start.
WhatBecause
Version the dataset alongside the model“Which examples produced this behaviour?” is unanswerable otherwise, and it is the first question when something is wrong.
Record base model, hyperparameters, and the commitReproducibility. A tuned model you cannot rebuild is a liability with a shelf life.
Run the same eval set as the base modelThe only way to state the improvement as a number rather than an impression.
Watch for regression on general abilityTuning hard on one task degrades others — catastrophic forgetting. Keep a few general cases in the eval set to catch it.
Keep the base model as a fallbackOne flag back to something that works if the tuned one misbehaves in production.
Diarise the base model's deprecationYour adapter is tied to a specific base. When it retires, you retrain — and you want that on a plan rather than as a surprise.
!
Re-run your evals when the base model updates. An adapter trained against one version of a base model can behave differently on the next, in ways nobody deployed and nobody expected. This is the same argument as pinning model versions in the AI Ops note, with more at stake: here the thing that shifted underneath you is something you paid to build.
✓ Worth remembering
  • Fine-tuning teaches manner, not facts. Facts belong in retrieval.
  • Try prompting and structured output first. Most fine-tuning projects should have stopped there.
  • LoRA for nearly everything: ~1% of the parameters, a small adapter, many variants on one base.
  • The dataset is the job. 500–1,000 consistent examples beat 50,000 inconsistent ones.
  • DPO over RLHF if you need preference tuning, and you may already be collecting the pairs.
  • Distillation is the strongest economic case — but do the arithmetic before, not after.
  • A tuned model is a versioned dependency you now maintain. Price that in.
