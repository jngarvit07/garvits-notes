First, sort out what is actually being compared

Most confusing conversations about AI happen because two people are comparing things at completely different levels. Asking “is Copilot better than Claude?” is a bit like asking whether a car is better than an engine.

💡
In plain English

A model is the engine. A platform is the showroom and the service centre. A product is the finished car you drive. GitHub Copilot is a car; Claude and GPT are engines that have been fitted into it at different times. Comparing them directly gives you an answer to a question nobody asked.

The model families

💡
In plain English
A handful of companies train the models that nearly everything else is built on. Each family has its own personality rather than a fixed rank. What actually differs between them is how much text they can hold, how they behave when calling tools, how they handle saying no, whether they are available in your country, and what the licence allows. Those are the things worth comparing, because they change what you can build.

Four groups matter in practice. Every one of them releases new versions every few months, so treat this as the general shape of things rather than a snapshot of who is ahead this week.

Where Copilot fits, and why the name confuses everyone

“Copilot” is a brand name Microsoft puts on several completely unrelated products. It is not a model, and the products sharing that name are very different from each other.

In plain English

Completion tools finish the line you are already typing. Agent tools take a paragraph describing what you want, go and do it across many files, and then show you exactly what changed. They suit different moments in the same working day, and most people who try both end up keeping both.

Choosing, without the horse race

💡
In plain English
Choose without treating it as a race. Benchmarks measure general ability on somebody else's problems, and the model that ranks third overall is very often first on the narrow thing you actually do. Your own small test set, built from your own real cases, beats any leaderboard on the only question that matters — which one is better for this.

The question “which model is best” has no answer at all. These questions do.

Real worldThree models in one product

Sorting incoming contact messages into spam or genuine is a narrow, clearly defined job: use a small fast model, called on everything, cheap enough that nobody notices it on the bill. Writing page copy is open-ended and the customer sees the result: use the top tier, called only now and then. Summarising a support conversation sits in between: use the balanced tier.

Picking one single model for the whole product is the common mistake. It shows up as a big bill rather than as a bug, which is exactly why it survives for so long.

Your team is on Azure with a signed commitment, and legal will not allow customer data to leave the tenancy. How much does model quality matter to the decision?Answer

Far less than the rule does. Start from what is actually available inside that boundary — which model families your platform offers, and under what data-handling terms — and pick the best option from that short list. Testing a model you are not allowed to send data to is time spent on a decision you cannot make.

The order is always the same: hard limits first, then your own test set, then cost. Benchmarks come last, if they come in at all.

Staying portable

💡
In plain English
The models change every few months. The way to survive that is not to pick the right one — it is to make the choice cheap to change later. One function in your codebase that every model call passes through turns “switch provider” from a rewrite into a config change, and it costs you about twenty lines of code.

Models get better, prices move, companies change their terms, and the best choice today will not be the best choice in eighteen months. The goal is not to choose correctly forever — it is to make being wrong cheap to fix.

PracticeWhat it buys you
Model ids in config, never inline in code.Switching becomes a deploy, not a rewrite. It also lets you lock a version in production while testing a new one beside it.
Prompts as files, not string literals.They get reviewed and rolled back like code — and prompts genuinely do need re-tuning when you change model.
An evaluation set you actually maintain.The only way to know that a switch actually made things better. Without it you are choosing on gut feeling and marketing blog posts.
A thin wrapper over the SDK call.One single place to change when a request looks different. Do not build a huge layer on top — the APIs are similar enough that a big one costs more than it saves.
Pin the exact model version in production.A newer version is better on average but it is not the same. Move on purpose, after running your tests — not just because it turned up.
!

Do not build too much on top. The temptation is a grand universal interface that supports every provider equally. In practice it settles at whatever the weakest provider can do, and you lose the very features you chose the model for — caching, thinking, structured outputs, the way tools work. A thin joining layer gives you portability. A whole framework gives you a second product to maintain.

✓ Worth remembering
  • Products, models and platforms are three different levels. Most confused comparisons are simply mixing up the levels.
  • Copilot is a brand name across several unrelated products, not a model. Ask which one, and running on what.
  • Who leads the benchmarks keeps changing, and it rarely predicts your result. Thirty real examples beat every leaderboard.
  • Hard limits — where data may live, cloud contracts, purchasing rules — usually settle this before quality gets a say.
  • Start with the smallest model and move up only where it fails. One product can sensibly use three.
  • Model ids in config, prompts in files, test sets kept up to date. Make being wrong cheap to fix, instead of trying to be right forever.
Why is being able to switch models quickly worth more than picking the best one today?Answer

Because today's ranking lasts a few months, while the code you write around it lasts years. A system that can change model in one afternoon gets the benefit of every improvement that comes along. One with provider-specific calls scattered all through it is stuck with a decision made at the moment you knew the least.

It is also your backup plan for outages. A provider having a bad day becomes a config change instead of an incident.
