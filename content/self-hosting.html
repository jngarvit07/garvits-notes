When to run a model yourself

💡
In plain English
Calling an API is like taking a taxi. Running your own model is like buying a car and maintaining it. The taxi is cheaper to start with, needs no effort, and somebody else worries about servicing — which is why it is the right answer for most teams most of the time. The cases where owning wins are real, and they are narrower and more specific than all the excitement suggests.
ReasonDoes it hold up?
Data is not allowed to leave our networkYes. A legal or contract rule, and no promise from a provider will satisfy it. The strongest reason there is.
Very high volume on one narrow taskYes, above a certain level. A small model keeping a GPU busy round the clock beats paying per token. Below that level, you are paying for a GPU that sits idle.
We need the model to never changeYes. Weights on your own disk do not change. A hosted model behind a name does change, and you control none of it.
Every millisecond countsSometimes. Skipping the network trip saves tens of milliseconds. Only worth it when the model call is already fast.
Heavy customisation of open weightsSometimes. Full fine-tuning, unusual decoding, direct access to the model's internals.
It will be cheaperUsually no. See below. This is the reason people give most often and the one that most often falls apart.
txtThe maths people skip
A capable open model on one cloud GPU, running non-stop:
    GPU instance                    ~$1-3 per hour
    x 24 x 30                       ~$700-2,200 / month
    + a second one for backup       x 2
    + storage, networking, data transfer
    + ENGINEER TIME: serving, upgrades, monitoring, being on call

  That is the floor. It costs the same whether you serve
  10 requests a day or 10,000.

  An API costs nothing when nobody is using it.

  So the real question is not "which is cheaper per token" but
  "do we have enough steady traffic to keep a GPU busy?"
  If a GPU would sit idle half the day, renting wins — and it
  keeps on winning for far longer than most teams expect.
!
Engineer time is the line that always gets left out. Somebody has to own the serving setup, answer the phone when a GPU node dies at 2am, test every model upgrade, and keep the driver and library versions matching each other. That is not a one-time setup cost; it is a permanent share of a person. Include it and the break-even point moves a long way further out.

Open weights, and what the licence actually says

💡
In plain English
"Open" here usually means you can download the numbers and run them. It rarely means you know what they were trained on, and it does not always mean you can use them in a business. The terms vary a lot between model families, and the differences are the kind that matter to whoever signs off on your product.
TermWhat it means
Open weightsYou can download the parameters. The training data and the training code usually are not included.
Open sourceProperly: weights, data and training code, under a real open-source licence. Genuinely rare.
Permissive licenceApache 2.0 or MIT. Commercial use, changes and redistribution all allowed. The easy case.
Custom / community licenceDownloadable, but with conditions — user-count limits, naming rules, restrictions on use, or a ban on training other models with the outputs. Read it.
Research onlyNot for a product, no matter how good the benchmark looks.
Check before you build, not after

Whether commercial use is allowed, whether there is a user-count limit, whether the outputs may train another model, and whether you must give credit. Ten minutes now, versus finding out during an investor's due-diligence review.

Pick by size class first

Roughly: 1–4B for sorting and field extraction on modest hardware; 7–14B the practical sweet spot for general work on one GPU; 30–70B getting close to hosted quality, at real infrastructure cost; 100B+ a serving project all by itself.

i
Model names and rankings change faster than anything written down, so this note deliberately names none. Pick a size class, shortlist two or three current models in it, and decide using your test set. A leaderboard measures general ability on somebody else's problems; your test set measures the only thing you actually care about.

Will it fit? The VRAM sum

💡
In plain English
A GPU has a fixed amount of fast memory, and the whole model has to live inside it. Each parameter takes a certain number of bytes depending on how precisely you store it, so the sum is: parameters times bytes, plus room for the conversation. Get this wrong and the model does not run slowly — it does not run at all.
txtThe formula, with a worked example
  VRAM  ~=  parameters x bytes-per-parameter  +  KV cache  +  overhead

  BYTES PER PARAMETER
    FP16 / BF16   2.0      full quality, the reference point
    INT8          1.0      ~half the memory, quality loss usually invisible
    INT4          0.5      ~a quarter, small but real quality loss

  A 7B MODEL
    FP16   7  x 2.0  = 14 GB   -> needs a 24GB card
    INT8   7  x 1.0  =  7 GB   -> fits a 16GB card comfortably
    INT4   7  x 0.5  =  3.5GB  -> fits an 8GB card, or a laptop

  A 70B MODEL
    FP16   70 x 2.0  = 140 GB  -> two 80GB cards
    INT4   70 x 0.5  =  35 GB  -> ONE 48GB card

  PLUS the KV cache, which grows with context length and with how many
  people are using it at once:
    ~0.5 GB per 1,000 tokens per simultaneous request (7B, FP16)
    32 users at once with 4,000 tokens each = ~64 GB of cache alone

  The cache is what people forget. It routinely gets bigger than the model.
!
Size your hardware for the number of simultaneous users, not for the model. A 7B model taking 14GB looks like it fits a 24GB card, right up until thirty people use it at once and the KV cache wants another 60GB. Batch size, context length and how many users at once are the three numbers that actually decide what you must buy — and a single-user test on your laptop tells you nothing about any of them.
A 13B model squeezed down to INT4 needs about 6.5GB. Why might it still fail on a 16GB GPU in real use?Answer

The KV cache. The weights fit easily, but every request happening at the same time holds its own cache, sized by its context length. Twenty users each with an 8,000-token conversation can need more memory than the model itself, and the failure shows up under load rather than in testing.

Your options: cap the context length, cap how many run at once, use a serving stack with paged attention that packs the cache tightly, or buy more memory. Discovering that you need one of those in the middle of a traffic spike is exactly what this sum exists to prevent.

Quantisation

💡
In plain English
Every parameter is stored to a certain number of decimal places. Quantisation keeps fewer of them — storing 0.7 instead of 0.7182 — so the model takes less memory and moves through the chip faster. The surprising finding is how little quality this costs: going from 16 bits to 8 is nearly free, and 4 is usually a good deal.
PrecisionMemoryQualityUse it
FP16 / BF16The baselineThe referenceWhen memory is not your limit
INT8~50%Almost impossible to tell apartA sensible default. Nearly free.
INT4~25%Small but measurable lossThe usual production choice. Especially good on bigger models.
Below 4-bit<25%You can notice it getting worseExperiments and very small devices
i
A big model squeezed down usually beats a small model at full precision for the same memory. A 70B at INT4 (~35GB) generally does better than a 13B at FP16 (~26GB) on hard tasks. If you are choosing between "bigger and squeezed" and "smaller and untouched" within a fixed memory budget, bigger and squeezed is the better first bet — then confirm it on your test set.
!
Quantisation hurts some things more than others, so measure it on your own task. The losses tend to show up first in long chains of reasoning, in arithmetic, in exact-format output, and in less common languages — while ordinary chat looks completely untouched. A casual test will tell you it is fine. Your test set will tell you whether it is fine for what you actually do.

Serving it properly

💡
In plain English
Loading a model and generating in a loop works for one user and falls apart for twenty. A real serving stack does two things that transform how much it can handle: it groups requests together so the GPU is never sitting idle, and it manages the KV cache in small pages so memory is not wasted on space that was booked but never used. Those two ideas are most of the difference.
StackBest forNotes
OllamaLaptops, prototypes, one userOne command and it runs. Excellent while developing, not built for many users at once.
llama.cppCPU, Apple Silicon, small devicesRuns where there is no GPU at all. Very good support for quantisation.
vLLMReal production serving on GPUsContinuous batching and paged attention. The default answer for serving at volume.
TGIProduction, if you live in the Hugging Face worldRoughly equal to vLLM. Choose whichever fits your existing tools.
TensorRT-LLMSqueezing the most out of NVIDIA hardwareFastest, and the most work to set up. For when the last 20% genuinely matters.
txtThe two ideas that make a serving stack fast
CONTINUOUS BATCHING
  simple way: [req A ......... done]
              wait            [req B ......... done]
              The GPU sits idle between them, and idle whenever A is short

  continuous: [A ......][B ....][C .........]
              [D ...........][E ......]
              A finishes -> a new request joins the batch IMMEDIATELY.
              The GPU never waits for a batch to fill up or empty out.

PAGED ATTENTION
  simple way: book the maximum context for every request, up front
              a 200-token chat holds 8,000 tokens of memory. Mostly wasted.

  paged:      hand out the cache in small blocks, only as needed
              (the same idea as virtual memory pages in an operating system)
              2-4x more simultaneous requests on exactly the same hardware.
bashAn OpenAI-compatible endpoint in one command
# Most stacks copy the OpenAI API shape, which means the SAME client
# code can point at either one — so this stays a config change, exactly
# as in the Architecture note. Do not write provider-specific calls.
vllm serve <model> \
  --quantization awq \
  --max-model-len 8192 \        # cap the context: this limits the KV cache
  --gpu-memory-utilization 0.90 \
  --max-num-seqs 32             # cap simultaneous requests: limits it again

# Your code, unchanged apart from the base URL:
#   baseURL: 'http://gpu-node.internal:8000/v1'
i
Serve it behind the same complete() wrapper as everything else. Self-hosting should be one line in a config file, so you can run a hosted model while developing and your own in production, or fall back to a hosted provider when a GPU node dies. Keeping that switch cheap is what keeps the decision reversible.

What you have just taken on

💡
In plain English
The API was not only running the model. It was handling capacity, backups, upgrades, scaling and being awake at 3am. Self-hosting moves all of that onto your team, and the list is longer than it looks from outside. This is not an argument against doing it — it is the list to cost out before you decide.
Now your jobWhat it involves
Capacity planningGPUs cannot scale down to zero and cannot be added in seconds. You buy for your busiest hour and pay for it at 4am too.
Cold startsLoading tens of gigabytes of weights takes minutes. Scaling up on demand is not realistic; you keep nodes warm.
FailoverOne node is a single point of failure. Two nodes plus a load balancer, or an outage you have agreed to accept.
Keeping versions matchedDriver, CUDA, framework and serving stack all have to agree with each other. Upgrades break in ways that are tedious to untangle.
Model upgradesDownload, quantise, test, shadow, roll out. The AI Ops discipline applies exactly the same.
The pagerA GPU node failing at 2am is now somebody's night.
MonitoringYou still need tokens, speed, failures and cost per feature — and now GPU usage, memory pressure and queue length as well.
Real worldThe practical split most teams end up with

The usual landing point is not all-or-nothing. The high-volume narrow tasks — sorting, extraction, embeddings, reranking — run on a small model you host yourself, because they are steady, they keep a GPU busy, and a small model does them well. The hard, low-volume, quality-sensitive work — the final answer a user reads — goes to a hosted top-tier model.

That split gets you most of the cost saving for a small fraction of the operational headache: one small model on one node is a far smaller commitment than serving a 70B with a backup. It also keeps the hosted model available as a fallback when your node is down.

Embeddings in particular are worth calling out. They are high-volume, the models are small and fast, quality differences on your own documents can be measured in an afternoon, and self-hosting keeps your documents inside your network. It is often the first thing a team brings in-house, and the easiest.

✓ Worth remembering
  • Data that cannot leave, weights that must never change, and very high steady volume all justify self-hosting. "It will be cheaper" usually does not.
  • Open weights is not open source, and the licence differences matter. Read it first.
  • VRAM = parameters × bytes + KV cache. The cache grows with context and with simultaneous users, and it is what people forget.
  • INT8 is nearly free; INT4 is the usual production choice. A big squeezed model beats a small untouched one at the same memory.
  • Use vLLM or TGI for production. Continuous batching and paged attention give you most of the throughput.
  • Expose an OpenAI-compatible endpoint so the choice stays a config change.
  • Many teams host the small high-volume models themselves and rent the big one. That split is usually the right shape.
