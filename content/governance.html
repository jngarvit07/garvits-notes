Why a developer should care about this

Governance sounds like somebody else's job — the legal team's, the compliance team's, some committee's. It lands on your desk for one simple reason: the duties attach to the system, and you are the only person who knows what the system actually does. Nobody else can tell an auditor what data went into the search index, or what the model is allowed to act on.

💡
In plain English

Governance is the paperwork that proves you were careful, plus the routine that makes you actually be careful. Most of it is not a legal question at all — it is simply writing down what the system does, what it must never do, who decided that, and how you would find out if it stopped being true.

i

The good news: if you have done notes {{n:evals}} and {{n:ai-security}} — a test set, a list of threats, traces, gates on one-way actions — you have already produced most of the evidence any framework asks for. Governance is mostly a matter of writing down what you did and keeping it up to date.

Risk tiers: the idea behind every framework

💡
In plain English
Every framework, whatever it is called, is built on one idea: the rules should get stricter as the possible harm gets bigger. A model suggesting the next song and a model screening job applications do not deserve the same level of checking. Working out which level you are in is most of the work, and it usually takes about ten minutes.

The rules differ in their details and agree on the main point: the duties grow with the possible harm, not with how clever the technology is. A model deciding which song plays next and a model deciding who gets a home loan are treated very differently, no matter how similar the code looks.

!

The level is set by what you use it for, not by the technology. The exact same model, the same prompt and the same code can be minimal risk in one product and high risk in another. A CV summariser is just a summariser; a CV summariser that ranks candidates is influencing who gets a job, and in the eyes of the law that is a completely different thing.

The frameworks you will hear named

💡
In plain English
You will hear three names. The EU AI Act is actual law, and it sorts systems into risk levels. NIST AI RMF is a voluntary framework in the US that describes a sensible way of working. GDPR is not about AI at all, but it applies the moment you handle personal data — which most AI features do. Knowing which one somebody means is half of understanding the question.
FrameworkWhat it isApplies when
EU AI ActReal, binding law using the risk levels above. It comes in stage by stage over several years, with real penalties.You sell a system in the EU, or its output is used in the EU — no matter where you yourself are sitting.
NIST AI RMFA voluntary US framework built around four activities: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage.Useful anywhere as a way of organising your work. Often what US customers ask you to follow.
ISO/IEC 42001A standard you can be formally certified against — the AI version of ISO 27001.Big companies' purchasing teams start asking for it once you sell to large organisations.
GDPR and similarData protection law. It came before the AI rules and applies to them in full.Any personal data anywhere — in training, in the search index, in prompts, or in logs. This one probably already applies to you.
Sector rulesBanking, medical, legal and employment regulators each have their own separate requirements.Often stricter and more detailed than the general AI rules. Check these before you look at the AI-specific ones.
i

This is a rough map, not legal advice, and the details keep changing — the EU AI Act in particular brings its rules in stage by stage over several years. When a real legal duty is at stake, the question goes to somebody properly qualified. What you need here is just enough to know which conversation you are in.

GDPR is the one that catches people first

QuestionWhere it bites in an AI system
What is your lawful basis for this processing?Personal data sitting in a search index counts as processing it. “It was already in our database” is not a valid reason for a brand new use.
Can you delete one person’s data on request?Easy in Postgres, harder in a vector index, and basically impossible to get out of a fine-tuned model. Plan for this before you fine-tune anything.
Where is it processed, and by whom?An API call sends data to another company, possibly to another country. That is a processing relationship, and it needs a contract.
How long do you keep prompts and outputs?Debug logs full of prompts are a store of personal data with nobody having set a deletion date. This is extremely common.
Is there a solely automated decision with legal effect?If yes, people have specific rights — including a genuine human review. A person who just signs off on everything is not a review.
!

Your logs are the quiet problem. Teams think very hard about the search index, and then log every prompt and every answer to some service forever, with no deletion policy at all. Prompts contain whatever users typed, which is very often personal and sometimes highly sensitive. Decide how long you keep them on purpose, and strip out the sensitive parts before writing, not afterwards.

Your feature summarises support tickets that contain customer names. Which of the three applies?Answer

GDPR, straight away and regardless of the AI part — you are handling personal data, so you need a lawful reason, a deletion date, and an answer for a deletion request that reaches your logs as well as your database.

The EU AI Act probably puts this in a low-risk level with light duties. The instinct to check the AI law first and the data-protection law second is usually back to front.

What this looks like in a repository

💡
In plain English
Governance sounds like a committee, and mostly it is not one. Inside a repository it is a handful of files: what this feature does and for whom, what data it touches and for how long, what it must not be used for, and what happens when it gets something wrong. One page for each feature answers most of what anybody will ever ask you.

Here is what that means in practice. None of it needs a committee, and all of it is exactly the evidence a review will ask you for.

A system card

One page for each AI feature: what it does, what it must never do, what data it touches, which model, who owns it, and what happens when it fails. Kept in the repository and reviewed like code.

Data lineage

What went into the index, where it came from, what right you had to use it, and when it should be deleted. “We are not really sure what is in there” is the answer that ends a sales conversation with a big customer.

Human oversight, specified

Which decisions need a person, what that person actually sees, and what they are able to change. Oversight that cannot change the outcome is not oversight at all.

Traces you can produce

For one particular decision on one particular day: the input, the text that was fetched, the model version, the output. This is the difference between explaining an incident and just apologising for one.

Measured accuracy

Your test scores, with dates, including the failures. “It works well” is not something anybody can accept; a tracked number with a stated method is.

Disclosure

Tell people when they are talking to a machine, and label content that was generated. It is cheap, more places require it every year, and it protects trust far more than it costs.

mddocs/ai/support-assistant.md — a system card that fits on a page
# Support assistant

**Owner:** platform team · **Reviewed:** 2026-08-14 · **Next review:** 2027-02-14

## What it does
Answers customer questions from our public help centre and the customer's own
account data. Drafts a reply; a support agent sends it.

## What it must not do
- Never send a reply without a human pressing send.
- Never access an account other than the one on the ticket.
- Never state a refund decision. It may explain the policy.

## Risk tier
Limited. It informs a human's reply; it makes no decision about a person.
Users are told they are reading an AI-drafted response.

## Data
- Help centre articles (public, ours).
- The requesting customer's own account record, scoped by session.
- Retention: prompts and outputs 30 days, redacted of card and address data.
- Basis: contract performance. DPA in place with the model provider.

## Model
`claude-sonnet-5`, pinned. Changes go through the eval suite first.

## How we know it works
`evals/cases/support-qa.json` — 68 cases. Last run 2026-08-14:
retrieval 94%, grounding 97%, correct refusal 100%.

## Failure modes and handling
- Not in the corpus -> explicit refusal, escalate to a human.
- Retrieval empty -> no draft offered at all, rather than a guess.
- Provider outage -> feature hidden; the queue works as it did before.
i

That page took twenty minutes to write and answers most of what any framework, auditor or large customer will ask. The reason it is believable is that every claim in it points at something real — a limited query, a pinned model id, a test file with an actual number in it.

Your CV-screening tool "only summarises" applications and a recruiter makes every decision. Is it high risk?Answer

Very likely yes, and “but a human decides” is a much weaker defence than it sounds. Employment is on the high-risk list, and what matters is whether the output seriously influences the decision. A summary that decides which applications ever get read has already made the decision that matters most.

The test for real human oversight is whether that person has the information, the time and the authority to reach a different conclusion. Somebody going through 400 summaries an hour is not overseeing the system; they are just approving it. If it is a rubber stamp, then treat the system as making the decision, because in practice that is exactly what it is doing.

Bias, and why it is an engineering problem

💡
In plain English
Bias is not a moral failing inside the model. It is something measurable in the outputs — and looking at it that way is what turns it into an engineering problem. If the system behaves differently for two groups who should be treated the same, that difference can be tested for, and that test can run on every single release.

A model learns from text that people wrote, and it repeats the patterns in that text — including the ones nobody wanted. Good intentions do not fix this, and reading the code will not reveal it.

Where it entersWhat to do about it
Training data reflects historical patterns.You cannot change this. What you can do is decide not to use a model for decisions where it matters, or measure the gap and act on it.
Your retrieval corpus over-represents some groups.Go and check what is actually in the index. Unlike the model, you can look inside this.
Your prompt carries assumptions.Cheap to test: run the same case with different names, genders and locations, and compare the results.
Your eval set only contains typical cases.Build cases specifically for the groups you might be treating unfairly. If they are not in the set, they cannot show up in the score.
i

The engineering move is to make it measurable. Take a set of cases, change only the one thing you are worried about, and compare the results. A gap you can put a number on is a bug you can go and fix; a gap you cannot measure is a gap you will just argue about.

▶
Practice

Write a system card for something you have already shipped. Give it twenty minutes and do not try to make it perfect.

The value is in the questions you cannot answer. “How long do we keep prompt logs?” and “what happens when the provider is down?” are usually the two that cause an awkward silence — and both are real gaps you can close this week.

  • The duties grow with the possible harm, and what you use it for sets the level — not the model, and not the design.
  • The one question that decides everything: what decision does this influence, and about whom?
  • GDPR probably already applies to you. Deletion, how long you keep things, and your lawful reason are the parts that bite.
  • Logs of prompts and answers are a store of personal data. Decide a deletion date on purpose, and strip sensitive parts out as you write them.
  • Human oversight only counts if that person could realistically reach a different conclusion.
