Where you are now

💡
In plain English
Before choosing what to learn next it is worth being accurate about where you are. Having read something is not the same as being able to build it without the tab open, and the gap between those two is where most people misjudge what they should do next.

If you have read the notes before this one, you know more than most people writing about AI on the internet. Specifically, you can now answer these — and knowing what you know is the precondition for choosing what to learn next.

What a model is doing

Next-token prediction, attention, context windows, why hallucination is structural rather than a bug to be patched.

How to give it knowledge

Embeddings, chunking, retrieval, grounding, citations — and where in that pipeline it actually goes wrong.

How to give it abilities

Tools, the agent loop, workflow patterns, MCP, skills — and the ladder that says which one to reach for.

How to know it works

Eval sets, graders, scoring retrieval and generation separately, wiring it into CI.

How it gets attacked

Prompt injection, the three-ingredient rule, and why architecture beats prompt wording every time.

What the rules are

Risk tiers, the frameworks by name, and the one-page artefact that answers most of what anyone asks.

!

Reading is not the same as knowing. The honest test for every item above is whether you have built something that used it and watched it fail. If not, that gap is the next thing to close — not the next note.

The path, in the order that works

Not a syllabus. Four stages, each with a thing you build, because the building is what moves the knowledge from recognisable to usable.

The foundations worth filling in

💡
In plain English
Some fundamentals keep paying out no matter which framework is fashionable: how HTTP actually works, what a database is doing, how to read a stack trace, git beyond commit and push, and how to write something down so another person can follow it. These age far better than any library.

You can build a great deal without these. They are what separates someone who can wire an API from someone who can debug why the wiring is not working.

You do not need to train a model. The instinct is to think real understanding starts with training, and for the overwhelming majority of applied work it is the least useful thing on this page. Fine-tuning is a specialist tool with a narrow set of cases; almost everything is prompting, retrieval, tools and evaluation.

Given where you already are — a working full-stack developer with a shipped product — AI engineering is the shortest path and the one that compounds fastest. You would be adding a layer to something you can already do, not starting again.

What is actually changing, and what is not

💡
In plain English
Most of what looks like rapid change is the same ideas being repackaged. Genuinely new capabilities arrive a few times a year; new names for existing ones arrive weekly. Being able to tell them apart is what stops you rewriting a working system every quarter.

Predicting AI is a poor use of anyone’s time. Directions that are already visible are a better guide than forecasts.

DirectionWhat it changes for you
Longer context, cheaperSome things RAG exists for stop needing RAG. Retrieval will still win on cost, freshness, permissions and citation — but the threshold moves.
Better reasoningMore tasks move from “not viable” to viable. Re-test the things you rejected a year ago; the answer changes.
Agents that run for hoursLong-horizon work becomes real, and everything in note 07 about budgets, gates and traces gets more important, not less.
StandardisationMCP and similar protocols mean less bespoke integration. Portability gets easier; lock-in gets weaker.
Regulation arrivingDocumentation and evaluation shift from good practice to requirement. Note 13 stops being optional.
Small models getting goodMore work runs locally, cheaply and privately. Routing gets more valuable, not less.
i

What has not changed in three years, and shows no sign of it: models predict text and cannot verify it; the quality of your data decides the quality of your system; evaluation is the only way to know anything; and the security problem is architectural. Anything you learn in those four areas keeps its value regardless of what ships next.

!

Do not chase releases. The pace makes it tempting to spend every week reading about the newest thing and never building anything. The people who get good pick a problem and stay with it while models improve underneath them. Depth compounds; novelty does not.

How to tell you have actually learned it

💡
In plain English
The honest test is not whether you followed the explanation — explanations are designed to be followable. It is whether you can build the thing from a blank file, and explain to somebody else why it is built that way. Everything short of that is recognition rather than knowledge.

A checklist that resists self-deception better than a course completion does. Each of these is a thing you have done, not a thing you have read.

You can genuinely say…Which proves
“I shipped an AI feature people use, and I know its failure rate.”You have met reality, and you measure rather than assume.
“I have an eval set, and I have watched a change make it worse.”You understand why evals exist, which only lands once one saves you.
“I found a retrieval bug I had blamed on the model.”You can localise a fault in a pipeline instead of tuning the prompt and hoping.
“I successfully injected my own agent, then made it impossible.”You understand that the fix is architectural.
“I cut cost significantly without hurting quality.”You understand caching and routing — and you had the eval to prove quality held.
“I explained to a non-technical person why it cannot be certain.”You understand the mechanism well enough to be honest about it, which is rarer than it should be.
▶
Practice — the only one that matters

Pick one real problem in something you already own. Not a tutorial, not a demo — a thing that annoys you or a customer weekly.

Build the smallest version that helps. Put it in front of one person. Write thirty eval cases from what they actually ask. Then spend a month making the number go up.

You will touch every note on this site doing that, in the order the problem demands rather than the order they are numbered — and at the end you will have both the understanding and something to point at.

✓ Worth remembering
  • Reading is not knowing. The test is whether you built something with it and watched it fail.
  • Ship small, add retrieval, measure, add tools, then run it in production. In that order.
  • Stage two — building the eval set — is where the real understanding forms and where most people quit.
  • Data handling is the highest-value foundation. You do not need to train a model.
  • AI engineering is the shortest path from where you already are, and it compounds fastest.
  • Four things have not changed in three years: models cannot verify, data quality decides quality, evaluation is the only proof, security is architectural. Learn those.

Reading a second time feels like progress and mostly reinforces recognition. Building is the only reliable test, which is why every note here ends by pointing at real code.
