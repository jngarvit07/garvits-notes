Note 10

The Model Landscape

Products, models and platforms are three different layers, and most confused comparisons are a layer mix-up. The families, where Copilot fits, and how to choose without benchmarks.

No experience needed 25 min read 5 sections

First, sort out what is actually being compared

Most confusing conversations about AI happen because two people are comparing things at different layers. “Is Copilot better than Claude?” is a bit like asking whether a car is better than an engine.

💡
In plain English

A model is the engine. A platform is the dealership and the service contract. A product is the finished car. GitHub Copilot is a car; Claude and GPT are engines it has been fitted with at various points. Comparing them directly produces an answer to a question nobody asked.

The model families

Four groups matter in practice. Every one of them ships new versions on a cadence of months, so treat this as the shape of the landscape rather than a snapshot of who is ahead this week.

Benchmark leadership rotates every few months and rarely predicts your result. A published score says a model did well on somebody else’s task. Thirty of your own real examples are worth more than every leaderboard put together — and building that set is the subject of note 11.

Where Copilot fits, and why the name confuses everyone

“Copilot” is a brand Microsoft applies across several unrelated products. It is not a model, and the products sharing the name differ enormously.

ProductWhat it actually is
GitHub CopilotA coding assistant in your editor — completions, chat, and agent modes. Runs on models from several vendors, and in recent versions you can pick which one.
Microsoft 365 CopilotAn assistant inside Word, Excel, Outlook and Teams, grounded in your organisation’s own documents and mail.
Copilot StudioA low-code builder for making your own assistants and connecting them to business systems.
Windows CopilotThe assistant built into the operating system.
i

The practical consequence: when a colleague says “Copilot got this wrong”, your first question is which Copilot, and on which underlying model. The answer often explains the result entirely.

In plain English

Completion tools finish the sentence you are already typing. Agentic tools take a paragraph of intent and go and do it across many files, then show you the diff. They suit different moments of the same day, and most people who use both keep both.

Choosing, without the horse race

The question “which model is best” has no answer. These do.

Real worldThree models in one product

Classifying inbound contact messages as spam or genuine is narrow and well defined: a small fast model, called on everything, cheap enough to ignore. Drafting page copy is open-ended and a customer sees the result: the frontier tier, called rarely. Summarising a support thread sits between them: the balanced tier.

Picking one model for the whole product is the common mistake. It shows up as a bill rather than a bug, which is why it survives so long.

Your team is on Azure with a signed commitment, and legal will not allow customer data to leave the tenancy. How much does model quality matter to the decision?Answer

Less than the constraint does. Start from what is actually available inside that boundary — which families your platform serves, and under what data-handling terms — and choose the best option from that shortlist. Evaluating a model you are not permitted to send data to is time spent on a decision you cannot make.

The order is always: constraints first, then your own evaluation set, then cost. Benchmarks come last, if at all.

Staying portable

Models improve, prices move, vendors change terms, and the best choice today is not the best choice in eighteen months. The goal is not to pick correctly forever — it is to make being wrong cheap.

PracticeWhat it buys you
Model ids in config, never inline in code.Switching is a deploy, not a refactor. It also lets you pin a version in production and test a new one alongside.
Prompts as files, not string literals.They get reviewed and reverted like code — and prompts genuinely need re-tuning between models.
An evaluation set you actually maintain.The only way to know a switch was an improvement. Without it you are choosing on vibes and vendor blog posts.
A thin wrapper over the SDK call.One place to change when a request shape differs. Do not build a heavy abstraction — the APIs are similar enough that a large one costs more than it saves.
Pin the exact model version in production.A newer version is better on average and not identical. Move deliberately, after running your evals — not because it appeared.
!

Do not over-abstract. The temptation is a grand provider-agnostic interface supporting every vendor equally. In practice it converges on the least capable common denominator, and you lose the features you chose the model for — caching, thinking, structured outputs, tool semantics. A thin seam is portability. A framework is a second product to maintain.

✓ Worth remembering
  • Products, models and platforms are three layers. Most confused comparisons are a layer mix-up.
  • Copilot is a brand across several unrelated products, not a model. Ask which one, and on what.
  • Benchmark leadership rotates and rarely predicts your result. Thirty real examples beat every leaderboard.
  • Hard constraints — residency, cloud commitments, procurement — usually decide this before quality does.
  • Start with the smallest model and step up only where it fails. One product sensibly uses three.
  • Model ids in config, prompts in files, evals maintained. Make being wrong cheap rather than trying to be right forever.
