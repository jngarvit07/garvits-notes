Full, LoRA, QLoRA

2 min read Some experience helps
💡
In plain English
A model is billions of numbers. Full fine-tuning adjusts all of them, which needs enormous hardware and produces an enormous new file. LoRA leaves them frozen and trains a small patch that sits alongside — typically under 1% the size — which turns out to work nearly as well for most tasks. It is the difference between reprinting a book and adding annotations to the margins.

txtWhy LoRA works, in one picture
  A layer's weights are a big matrix W  (say 4096 x 4096 = 16.7M numbers)

  FULL FINE-TUNE:   train a new W        16,700,000 numbers changed

  LoRA:             keep W frozen
                    train A (4096 x 8)   and   B (8 x 4096)
                    use  W + (A x B)

                    32,768 + 32,768  =   65,536 numbers changed
                                          ~0.4% of the layer

  The insight: the ADJUSTMENT a task needs is far simpler than
  the knowledge already in W, so it compresses into a thin pair.
i
The multi-tenant property is the one people miss. Because an adapter is small and the base is shared, one served model can carry dozens of adapters in memory and pick per request. A different tuned behaviour per customer becomes economically ordinary rather than absurd — with full fine-tuning it would be one entire model per customer.

The knobs, and sensible starting values

SettingStart atWhat it does
rank (r)16How much capacity the patch has. 8–16 for style and format; 32–64 for a genuinely new skill. Higher is not better by default.
alpha32 (2× rank)How strongly the patch is applied.
learning rate1e-4 to 2e-4Higher than full fine-tuning, because far fewer parameters are moving.
epochs2–3More than 3 on a small set overfits reliably. Watch the validation loss, not the training loss.
target modulesattention projectionsAdding the feed-forward layers helps on harder tasks and costs memory.
