Attention, explained properly

2 min read Read the AI note first
💡
In plain English
Read this sentence: “The trophy would not fit in the suitcase because it was too big.” To understand “it”, you glanced back at “trophy” rather than “suitcase”. Attention is that glance, made mechanical: every token looks at every earlier token, scores how relevant each one is, and builds its own understanding as a weighted blend of them.

Why this was the breakthrough

Before attention, models read text in order, one word at a time, carrying a summary forward. Information from twenty words ago had to survive twenty hand-offs, and mostly did not. Attention lets any token reach any other token directly, in one step, no matter how far apart they are. That is what “Transformer” names, and it is the whole reason long-range understanding works.

It also gave a second gift: because every token is scored against every other independently, the work parallelises across thousands of chips. Reading in order cannot be parallelised. The reason models got large enough to be surprising is as much this as it is the idea itself.

The cost, and why it shapes everything downstream

txtEvery token attends to every token
   1,000 tokens  ->  1,000,000 pairs
  10,000 tokens  ->  100,000,000 pairs        100x the work for 10x the text
 100,000 tokens  ->  10,000,000,000 pairs

Work grows with the SQUARE of the input.
!
This is why long context is expensive rather than merely priced that way. Doubling your prompt does not double the cost of processing it. It is also why “just put everything in the context window” is not the free alternative to retrieval that it appears to be — you pay in latency and money on every single call, forever, for documents that were mostly irrelevant to that particular question.
Why can a model with a two-million-token context window not simply replace your retrieval system?Answer

Three reasons, in increasing order of importance. Cost and latency scale badly, so every question pays for every document. Attention gets diluted — performance on finding a specific fact degrades as the surrounding material grows, which is the “lost in the middle” effect. And your corpus is usually far larger than any window anyway.

The useful reframing is that long context and retrieval solve different problems. Retrieval decides what is relevant; context length decides how much relevant material fits. A big window makes retrieval more forgiving — you can send twenty passages instead of five — but it does not remove the need to choose.
