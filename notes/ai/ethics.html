Bias, law and telling people

3 min read No experience needed
💡
In plain English
Three practical duties, not a philosophy lecture. Do not let the system make decisions about people that you could not explain or defend. Follow the law wherever you operate — and the law now has specific things to say about AI. And tell people when they are talking to a machine, because most of the anger these systems create comes from people feeling they were fooled about that.

These systems are trained on text written by people, and they repeat what is in it — including the parts nobody would stand behind. This is not a side note about morals; it is a product risk with legal consequences, and it turns up in very ordinary features.

  • Bias comes along for the ride, and you cannot see it. A model asked to shortlist job applicants, rank complaints or judge someone's tone will carry the patterns of its training data. If a decision affects a person's money, job, housing or health, the model is an input to a human decision — never the decision itself.
  • An explanation is not a reason. Ask why it answered that way and you get a nice-sounding story, produced the same way the answer was. It is a guess, not a record of what happened. Where you need a real reason, use rules that you wrote yourself.
  • Copyright is still unsettled. Training data, generated output and who owns it are all being fought over in court in several countries. For a small product the practical rules are boring and enough: do not ask for imitations of a living artist or a specific brand, keep generated pictures out of your logos and trademarks, and read what your provider promises to cover.
  • Rules and regulations now exist. The EU AI Act brings in duties step by step depending on how risky your use is, and several countries require you to say when a person is talking to a machine. If you operate in the EU, working out which category your feature falls into is a question for the start of the project, not the end.
  • Privacy law applies exactly as before. Personal data inside a prompt is data processing. It needs a legal basis, an agreement with the processor, an answer about how long it is kept, and a line in your privacy notice.
i
Say that it is AI. Label generated content, make it obvious when a reply did not come from a person, and give people a way to reach a real one. More places require this every year, and it is the difference between a user forgiving a wrong answer and feeling cheated by it.

One more point that gets less attention: the environmental and human cost is real, but it is not the one people usually talk about. Training is expensive and happens once. Your feature's share is inference, repeated millions of times. Caching, smaller models, and not calling the model at all when a simple rule would do are the levers — and they are the very same levers that make your bill smaller.
